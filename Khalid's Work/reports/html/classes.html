Methodology note

Consumption-Based Social Classes in Pakistan

How the five classes and their thresholds are defined, using HIES 2024-25

DataHIES 2024-25, Pakistan Bureau of Statistics
Sample30,123 households; four provinces and Islamabad
Unit of analysisHousehold (40.1 million, weighted)
DateNovember 2026

Summary

  • Households are ranked by spending per person and divided into five classes by K-means clustering.
  • The thresholds are $3.39, $5.51, $9.25 and $19.49 per person per day, in 2023 PPP dollars.
  • 62% of households are lower-middle or middle class; 4.2% (1.7 million households) are upper class.

1How the classes are made

  1. Add up each household's spending for one month. This includes everything the household consumed: what it bought, food it grew itself, the rent value of a house it owns, and goods received as wages or gifts. Items asked about over 14 days or 12 months are converted to one month.
  2. Convert to dollars per person per day. Divide by the number of household members, so a large family is not ranked as rich just because it spends more in total; then by 30.4 days and by Rs 67.96, the value of one 2023 PPP dollar at 2024-25 prices (the World Bank’s 2023 PPP rate for household consumption, Rs 59.28, raised by the 14.6% price rise to 2024-25).
  3. Let K-means find five groups. K-means places five centres along the spending scale and puts every household, counted with its weight, with its nearest centre. Spending is compared on a ratio scale, so “twice as much” is the same step for poor and rich.
  4. Read off the thresholds. The boundary between two neighbouring classes lies halfway between their centres on that ratio scale. This is the square root of the two centres multiplied together: for example √(2.63 × 4.36) = $3.39.
  5. Count the households in each class by adding up their weights.

3Checks

  • Same result every time. K-means is run from 50 random starts and gives the same classes; the thresholds in Table 1 reproduce 100% of them.
  • Income gives the same picture. Classes by income per person have similar thresholds ($3.36, $5.70, $10.01, $21.96); 68% of households keep their class, 98% stay within one class.
  • Totals match the official report. Average household spending and income are within 3% of the PBS HIES 2024-25 report (2.8% and 2.3% higher).

4Reading the thresholds

  • They are spending per person, not salaries. A family of six is upper class only if it spends more than 6 × $19.49 = $117 a day. One earner on $20 a day supporting six people gives $3.33 per person: lower class. The main earner provides only 50–54% of household income.
  • Spending includes imputed consumption (Table 2): 18.5% of household spending is not paid in cash. Poorer classes rely more on own produce and gifts, the upper class on the rental value of its homes. With cash spending only and the same thresholds, the lower class would be 32% of households instead of 17%, the upper class 2.7% instead of 4.2%, and only 66% of households would keep their class.
  • Limits. Spending is not wealth, prices differ between regions, and the K-means thresholds apply to this survey round only; for comparisons over time, hold the 2023 PPP dollar thresholds fixed. The $2/$4/$6 lines of the Middle and Affluent Class analysis are in 2011 PPP dollars and not directly comparable.

5Urban and rural households classified separately

Urban: 10,960 sampled households (16.3 million); rural: 19,163 (23.8 million). Same K-means method as steps 3–4, weighted by the household weight.

  • The class structure is the same. Classified on their own, urban households split 19 / 34 / 27 / 15 / 5% and rural households 16 / 31 / 29 / 18 / 5% (lower to upper), close to the national 17 / 32 / 30 / 17 / 4%.
  • The dollar levels are not. Urban thresholds are 27–32% above the national ones and rural thresholds 11–31% below. The urban/rural ratio rises from 1.4 at the bottom to 1.9 at the top, so higher urban prices alone cannot explain the gap.
  • With the national thresholds, 23% of rural households are lower class and 1.9% upper class, against 8.5% and 7.6% of urban households. 56% of urban and 67% of rural households keep their national class when classified within their area.
  • Which to use. HIES has no regional price index, so part of the gap is the higher urban cost of living. Use the national thresholds for a Pakistan-wide picture, the area thresholds to compare households with their neighbours.

Imputed consumption differs in kind. In rural areas it is 21.6% of spending, mostly own produce, and stays at 21–22% in every class: richer rural households grow less of their food but own more valuable homes. In cities it is 15.4%, mostly the rental value of owned homes, and rises from 12% in the lower class to 24% in the upper class (national classes).
