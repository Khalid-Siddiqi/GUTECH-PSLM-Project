Methodology note

Consumption-Based Social Classes in Pakistan

How the five classes and their thresholds are defined, using HIES 2024-25

DataHIES 2024-25, Pakistan Bureau of Statistics
Sample30,123 households; four provinces and Islamabad
Unit of analysisHousehold (40.1 million, weighted)
DateNovember 2026

Summary

  • Households are ranked by spending per person and divided into five classes at $3, $6, $10 and $20 per person per day, in 2023 PPP dollars. These lines are the K-means thresholds rounded.
  • 71% of households are lower-middle or middle class; 4.0% (1.6 million households) are upper class.
  • Ownership rises steeply with class: 52% of upper-class households own a car and 62% an air conditioner, against 5% and 10% in the middle class.

1How the classes are made

  1. Add up each household's spending for one month. This includes everything the household consumed: what it bought, food it grew itself, the rent value of a house it owns, and goods received as wages or gifts. Items asked about over 14 days or 12 months are converted to one month.
  2. Convert to dollars per person per day. Divide by the number of household members, so a large family is not ranked as rich just because it spends more in total; then by 30.4 days and by Rs 67.96, the value of one 2023 PPP dollar at 2024-25 prices (the World Bank’s 2023 PPP rate for household consumption, Rs 59.28, raised by the 14.6% price rise to 2024-25).
  3. Let K-means find five groups. K-means places five centres along the spending scale and puts every household, counted with its weight, with its nearest centre. Spending is compared on a ratio scale, so “twice as much” is the same step for poor and rich.
  4. Read off the thresholds. The boundary between two neighbouring classes lies halfway between their centres on that ratio scale. This is the square root of the two centres multiplied together: for example √(2.63 × 4.36) = $3.39. The four K-means thresholds are $3.39, $5.51, $9.25, $19.49.
  5. Round them to fixed class lines: $3, $6, $10, $20. Round lines are easier to use and, being fixed in 2023 dollars, can be applied unchanged to other survey rounds. 85% of households are in the same class as under the exact K-means thresholds.
  6. Count the households in each class by adding up their weights.

2Checks

  • Same result every time. K-means is run from 50 random starts and gives the same classes; its thresholds reproduce 100% of them, and the rounded lines keep 85% of households in the same class.
  • Income gives the same picture. Ranked by income per person with the same lines, 72% of households keep their class and 98% stay within one class.
  • Totals match the official report. Average household spending and income are within 3% of the PBS HIES 2024-25 report (2.8% and 2.3% higher).

4Reading the class lines

  • They are spending per person, not salaries. A family of six is upper class only if it spends more than 6 × $20 = $120 a day. One earner on $15 a day supporting six people gives $2.50 per person: lower class. The main earner provides only 50–54% of household income.
  • Spending includes imputed consumption (Table 2): 18.5% of household spending is not paid in cash. Poorer classes rely more on own produce and gifts, the upper class on the rental value of its homes. With cash spending only and the same thresholds, the lower class would be 25% of households instead of 12%, the upper class 2.6% instead of 4.0%, and only 69% of households would keep their class.
  • Limits. Spending is not wealth and prices differ between regions. K-means thresholds would move with each survey round; the fixed lines do not, so they can be used for comparisons over time after converting each round's spending to 2023 PPP dollars. The $2/$4/$6 lines of the Middle and Affluent Class analysis are in 2011 PPP dollars and not directly comparable.

  • A mobile phone and an electric fan are near-universal: 92% and 81% of households own them even in the lower class.
  • A refrigerator, washing machine and television mark the middle class: each is owned by 64–76% of middle-class households but only 18–22% of lower-class ones.
  • A car and an air conditioner mark the top: 52% and 62% of upper-class households own them, against 5% and 10% of the middle class. Ghani (2014) found the same ladder in 2010-11: fan, mobile and TV, then washing machine and fridge, then motorcycle, then car and AC.
  • Not everything rises with class: sewing machines and motorcycles level off above the middle class, and tractors are rare in every class (at most 4%).

  • Compared with all of Pakistan (A), the areas differ. City households spend more, so they sit higher: 7.3% of urban households are upper class against 1.8% of rural ones, and 16% of rural households are lower class against 5.4% of urban ones.
  • Compared only with their own area (B), they split the same way, close to the national K-means split of 17 / 32 / 30 / 17 / 4%. Inequality within cities and within villages has the same shape; only the level differs. Urban thresholds are 1.4 times the rural ones at the bottom and 1.9 times at the top, so higher urban prices alone cannot explain the gap.
  • Which to use. HIES has no regional price index, so part of the gap is the higher urban cost of living. Use A for a Pakistan-wide picture and B to compare households with their neighbours.
